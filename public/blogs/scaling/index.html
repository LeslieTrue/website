Careful Scaling in Academia

🐼: “Tianzhe, don’t kill my job until it reaches SOTA.”

Tianzhe Chu

    This blog is written after months of suffering co-managing the HKU ICRC cluster and finishing the Nestor paper. In a resource-limited place, battling for compute is quite common — and nothing triggers anger quite like waiting in the queue while watching someone burn GPUs on a 5% MFU job. University compute is usually funded by the government, so that’s literally your papa and mama’s tax money going up in smoke. This post is the basic education I wish everyone in Yi’s group had: enough to prompt their agent sensibly, or at least to have some sense of the cost, before crying to me for more chips :<

    Basically the blog has 3 parts

    • Science part, some basics on what a scaling ladder is
    • Bitter lesson part, on how to actually apply it in your research
    • Checklist before asking for chips

    Science part: what it is and why we want it

    There are many interesting lectures or notes on what a scaling ladder is and you may find details there []. Here I just want to briefly mention it — given a set of at least 4 exp logs with different compute budgets and from the same model family, you fit a curve

    L(C)=AC−α+L∞

    This curve can help do two things — 1. predict the larger scale perf that you cannot afford (or verify their perf if you can); 2. compare two model families’ scaling ladders to see which actually works better. This style of comparison gives some 不烧心 (peace of mind) before burning chips, cuz the training compute of any arch can be calculated in theory (or sometimes plus a small run for MFU estimation if you want the actual wall time as the x axis).

    Inspired by Tatsu’s course at Stanford, I came up with another academia view on why we want it:

    Scaling ladder as a structured prediction objective for PhD training

    Let’s now think of this from an inverse view — say, instead of you training neural networks, actually the training experiment logs are training your brain.

    Definition 1 Predict one experiment

    y^=fθ(d,h,C)(1)
    minθ‖fθ(d,h,C)−y‖22(2)
    d
    Data configuration.
    h
    Architecture and hyperparameter configuration.
    C
    Training compute budget, measured in FLOPs.
    ŷ, y
    Predicted and measured model performance, respectively.
    fθ
    Your mental predictor; θ represents what you learn from experiment logs.

    Your exp logs give you the input (data, arch config, compute) and the label (model perf) and your PhD career is minimizing the objective like equation 2. However, model perf alone is not that learnable (i.e. it may not have a distribution) while data, arch config (hparams) may have tons of combinations to try.

    QHow to make this learning process more efficient?

    AUse scaling ladder, or at least think of your exps with a sense of it.

    Definition 2 Predict a scaling curve

    gθ(dτ,ℋ,𝒞)=(A,α,L∞)
    L(C)=AC−α+L∞
    dτ
    A shared data recipe with fixed tokens per parameter, τ (TPP), across the ladder.
    ℋ
    A constrained architecture family, for example with fixed sparsity and aspect ratio.
    𝒞
    The set of training compute budgets along the ladder; C is one such budget.
    gθ
    Your mental predictor of the whole scaling curve.
    A, α, L∞
    The curve’s amplitude, scaling exponent, and asymptotic loss floor.

    Here, both the input and prediction target change. Instead of the free-form data config, the scaling ladder enforces a fixed TPP budget; on the arch config side, it should also follow constraints like fixed sparsity, fixed aspect ratio, etc. The prediction target, on the contrary, is reduced from O(N) scaling data points to 3 variables — A, α, and L∞. This design augments the training data by strengthening correlations between data points, while reducing the complexity of the prediction. Too good. I can’t imagine what my panda would be like if he were trained with this kind of training data.

    Bitter lessons to actually run a scaling ladder exp

    To actually apply a scaling ladder in your research, you may have to instantiate two things: the x-axis and the y-axis, where the x-axis is usually FLOPs while the y-axis is quite tricky if you are not working on language modeling. My advisor is a vision guy and was a bit reluctant to switch to language but was convinced by my evidence — yea, never try to run a scaling ladder on 300-epoch ImageNet with classification acc as the y-axis. It’s miserable. The following part would presume that you are working on language modeling (or at least using NTP to train your visual models, or you already invented a perfect y-axis for your domain).

    Configuration

    Once you’ve settled on your y-axes, the next question is how to pick your data points. Common choice is distributing the model sizes roughly by 1x, 2x, 4x, 8x to make them land beautifully on a scaling curve (it’s obvious for the exponential law). Second, make sure your model is not undertrained. According to Chinchilla, 20 TPP is the minimum requirement to sufficiently train a model, but often more is better — and usually MoE consumes more tokens if you calculate TPP by active params. Some works have some theories on the right number [], but yea, as you are in academia, don’t choose a too large one. Nestor picked 50 TPP with the denominator as the active number of params.

    During setting the model archs, you may have another concern that makes you 挠头: arch variants often change the actual FLOPs of the model. Say if you want to compare arch A and arch B where B has a slight modification i.e. on the attention part, sparse attention reduces the FLOPs. Although a scaling ladder does not require A and B to have matched FLOPs point by point, it's still worth thinking whether you should compensate for the drop by adding more dims / more layers / more heads, etc. My personal take is, yes, but still make sure your tuned arch has slightly smaller FLOPs than the baseline, i.e. 0.95x-0.99x. Under this setting, direct perf comparison on this data point would be meaningful - i.e. you can 不烧心地 promote B if it achieves on par or slightly better perf than A without running the full scaling ladder.

    MFU

    After configuring everything, you are going to launch your real training exps on GPUs. Now it’s agent time and you can just prompt “go f**king run the exps and maximize MFU.” But this alone usually doesn’t work well — an example is Nestor’s native kernels were beaten by Twitter folks easily []. Here are two lessons from my suffering:

    Parallelism is scale-dependent. My exp logs documented the following fun stuff. In one 200M scale exp of Nestor, optimizer-state sharding made the optimizer update slower; while things flipped at 1B, where the sharding helped unlock a larger per-GPU microbatch. A general takeaway is, fancy sharding helps MFU on large models but for smaller ones, you have to tolerate the number and keep reading.

    Pay attention to the actual computation per step. GPUs generally run large, regular matrix multiplications more efficiently than many small ones. Splitting the same arithmetic into lots of tiny matmuls can underutilize the GPU and introduce launch bubbles between kernels. MoE and Nestor are two perfect examples here — let’s first forget about the load balancing issues, MoE slices the FFN matrices, and Nestor in addition slices the attention projections. This makes 3 equal-FLOPs models have an obvious MFU ranking: dense > MoE > Nestor, though advanced infra magic may help close the gap especially on larger scales. Beyond the compute itself, communication can bite you too. In an earlier multi-node run of Nestor, gradients were supposed to be synchronized only once per optimizer step. But after torch.compile wrapped the model, the helper function failed to recognize the DDP instance and silently skipped no_sync, so gradients were actually synchronized after every microbatch. Meanwhile, the network configuration funneled cross-node traffic through a single network port.

    All the above experiences came from communicating with agents. Though Claude may have way stronger infra skills than me, I need to nudge it forward if I find the MFU value suspicious or the decomposed actual computation suspicious.

    Bottleneck may not be chips' compute

    Bottleneck may not be chips especially for vision / robot folks. it maybe data loading, simulator rendering, etc; for RL guys, it maybe the speed of inference engine, RL algorithms, distribution of # training enginers and # inference engines. My panda 信誓旦旦 spoke "I cant run my exps without 32 h200s cuz it would be too slow" but the actual hidden reason for slowing is not the number of chips - used to random shuffle every time for each data sample and face some filelock issues when reading video data.

    Checklist before asking for chips

    should you keep working on this project?

    Let's propose a metric: cost-effective research score = impact / ( λice * gpu hrs) where the gpu hrs is those experiments actually kept in main paper In academia; λice * gpu hrs denote the total gpu hrs including those dropped or hidden by yourself. your goal should be optimizing this metric instead of optimizing impact alone overlooking the burnt gpu hrs.     What's your smallest exp gpu hr requirement?

    Say your group has # of gpu K, with m active users, your fair share would be K/m.

    A reasonable smallest exp would be ≤ K/m × 1/4 running for within one day to allow you for enough sweeping. You then should follow a strict promotion rule i.e. escalate part of your successful exps to 1/2 K/m gpus → K/m gpus → asking more from your advisor to use more than fair share → asking me or Yi for temporary more quota.
