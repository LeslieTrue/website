Careful Scaling in Academia

🐼: “Tianzhe, don’t kill my job until it reaches SOTA.”

Tianzhe Chu

    Introduction

    Scaling studies are routine in frontier labs and rare in academia, and compute is only part of the reason. Most people running them at a university are junior. Some have never touched a Titan X, a 1080, or a V100, and start straight on H200s, where they burn hours on jobs a much smaller chip could run, with no feel for how long an experiment should take. Some YOLO a run on as many GPUs as they can grab. Some have no sense of a fair comparison and keep training until the number reaches SOTA. This post is the basic education I wish they had: enough to prompt their agent sensibly, or at least to have some sense of the cost, before crying to me for more chips. What follows comes from my own experience working on Nestor and co-managing the HKU ICRC cluster.

    Science: control the variables

    One goal of empirical deep learning research in academia is to train the PhD student's brain into a prediction function:

    L(H, D, M, C, T) → model performance

    Given hyperparameters (H), data (D), model capacity (M), compute (C), and wall time (T), can you predict what a run will achieve before you launch it? Stanford's Deep Learning Alchemy course puts it simply: “Prediction is understanding” [16]. Your experiments are the training data for this prediction function.

    How should you organize that training data? A full grid over n variables with k choices each needs kn runs. Three learning rates, four data sizes, and three model sizes already make 36 runs per method. Researchers usually pick a few variables, narrow their ranges, and fix the rest by intuition. What they learn from these runs can be hard to carry over to a new scale.

    A scaling ladder gives you something simpler to learn. Make compute the main axis and specify how model size, data, and hyperparameters vary along it. Each method then becomes a performance curve across compute budgets [3][4].

    Compare the baseline and the proposed method along matched ladders. Their curves show the performance gain at fixed compute, the compute saving at fixed performance, and how these benefits change with scale.

    Match tuning budgets [1], data, and data order at each rung, and use the same compute accounting and stopping rules. Tuning matters: a well-tuned LSTM once beat fancier language models [2].

    Measure the x-axis: compute

    To draw a scaling curve, we need to count each run's training FLOPs. A common estimate is C ≈ 6ND [3]. Here N counts the weights in the Transformer's dense attention projections and MLPs, and D counts all training tokens processed, including repeated passes over the data. We will count the vocabulary head separately.

    Where does the six come from? Count each multiply-add as two FLOPs: one for the multiplication and one for the addition. Consider a linear layer Y = XW, with X of shape m × a and W of shape a × b. Let G be the gradient at its output. Training needs three matrix multiplications:

    Leading FLOPs for one linear layer, with both gradients required.
    OperationProductFLOPs
    ForwardXW2mab
    Input gradientGWT2mab
    Weight gradientXTG2mab

    That is 6ab FLOPs per token. Sum the matrix sizes over the model to get 6N, then multiply by D. This assumes each counted weight is used once per token and that both gradients are computed. It leaves out attention between tokens and smaller operations such as normalization and activations.

    The actual FLOPs can differ from this estimate. Dense attention's compute per sequence grows quadratically with sequence length, and a looped Transformer reuses the same parameters several times in one forward pass. Both add compute without adding parameters, which 6ND alone does not capture.

    For standard multi-head attention, counting the full square attention matrix, the training matmul FLOPs are approximately [17]:

    C ≈ 6ND + 12ℓDsd.

    Here N counts projection and MLP weights, D is the total number of training tokens, ℓ is the number of layers, s is the sequence length, and d is the hidden width.

    Say which attention convention you use. A causal kernel that skips masked pairs roughly halves the attention term, giving 6ℓDd(s + 1) in the ideal triangular count. Applying a mask after computing the full matrix saves no matmul work. FlashAttention improves memory use and execution efficiency, but dense attention still does quadratic arithmetic per sequence, and backward recomputation adds work beyond this count [18].

    Linear attention removes this dependence on sequence length. Gated DeltaNet [19] keeps a dv × dk state per head. Each token decays this state, updates the value stored for its key, and reads an output with its query. These matrix-vector products and outer products cost Θ(hdkdv) per token for h heads. The recurrent mixing cost over ℓ layers and D tokens is therefore Θ(ℓDhdkdv), independent of sequence length. With equal head widths dh and d = hdh, this becomes Θ(ℓDddh), compared with Θ(ℓDsd) for softmax attention.

    With total tokens, head dimensions, and chunk size fixed, Gated DeltaNet's mixing FLOPs stay roughly constant as sequences get longer. This cost is still missing from 6ND, so count it alongside the projections and MLPs.

    Match FLOPs or not?

    Two scaling curves do not need points at exactly the same FLOPs. In practice, though, I keep the candidates at each rung close in compute, so that every run helps me decide what to train next. With a limited budget, this lets me compare validation performance at roughly the same cost and carry only the most promising configurations up to the next scale.

    Keeping these budgets close takes some adjustment when a design change makes a module cheaper or more expensive. If we want to train on the same number of tokens, we can make up the difference by changing the depth, the hidden width, or the number of attention heads and the head dimension.

    These adjustments can cost GPU efficiency. Dimensions divisible by 128 often work well, depending on the hardware, precision, and kernel [20], but a closer FLOP match may force less efficient shapes. This is a trade-off between comparable budgets and throughput. Whichever way you go, plot each run at its actual compute. We will come back to choosing model dimensions with the hardware in mind in the engineering section.

    Measure the y-axis: validation performance

    Once we can count compute, we need a metric worth predicting. For autoregressive language models, validation cross-entropy is the closest thing to a gold standard: it measures how well the model predicts unseen tokens. Perplexity is the same quantity in a different form, PPL = exp(loss), with loss in nats per token. Keep the validation data, tokenizer, and context protocol fixed across the ladder [21]. In vision and robotics, finding an equally useful testbed and metric still calls for more exploration.

    I don't have much insight to offer here, but I do have one bitter lesson: never use ImageNet classification accuracy after 300 epochs as the y-axis of your scaling ladder. I tried it. It was miserable.

    Fit the scaling curve

    Each completed run gives us one point: training compute and validation loss. In Nestor, we fit each architecture's points with the same form:

    L(F) = A F−α + L∞.

    Here F is training compute, A sets the scale of the reducible loss, α controls how fast it falls, and L∞ is the fitted loss floor. For each candidate α, we solve for A and L∞ by linear least squares, then keep the exponent with the smallest squared error in loss.

    To compare compute at a target loss L*, invert the fit: F(L*) = [A / (L* − L∞)]1/α, for L* > L∞. Trace horizontally from a measured Nestor point to a baseline's curve, then divide the baseline's estimated compute by Nestor's actual compute. At the largest rung, this gives about 1.22× for MoE and 4.1× for the dense Transformer. Both numbers rely on extrapolating past the largest measured baseline run.

    Four points for three fitted parameters means a close fit alone is weak evidence that the curve extrapolates. Treat L∞ as a fitted offset, not a known asymptote, and check the prediction against a larger run when you can.

    Behind the scaling curve

    Under construction

    A point on the scaling curve is a pair of numbers, compute and loss. Everything else about the run is hidden. If two ladders hide different things, their curves are not comparable, and the gap between them is not a result. This section lists what I hold fixed or scale by a stated rule at every rung. We already covered one item, keeping candidates close in FLOPs at each rung. The rest are below, in the order you set them up: data, schedule, parametrization, optimizer.

    Tokens per parameter

    Rule: fix the tokens-per-parameter ratio along the ladder, or fix how it changes, and apply the same choice to every candidate.

    Under the 6ND approximation, compute is proportional to model size times tokens, so one compute budget can be spent many ways. A curve where each rung trains for 20 tokens per parameter and a curve where each rung trains for 200 are both valid, but they are different curves, and a candidate that wins on one can lose on the other. Chinchilla [4] found a compute-optimal ratio of roughly 20 tokens per parameter for dense Transformers in its setting. Models intended for extensive inference often train for far more than that to favor a smaller model, and scaling laws have also been observed on these over-trained ladders [25].

    Say which N you use for the ratio. For a dense model it is the body parameter count from section 2.1. For mixture-of-experts it is ambiguous: tokens per active parameter and tokens per total parameter differ according to the routing sparsity and the share of non-expert parameters, and the choice changes where the MoE rung lands relative to the dense one. State it once and keep it.

    Schedule and training length

    Rule: use the same warmup, decay shape, and final learning rate on every ladder, with the decay set to each run's actual length.

    The schedule interacts with training length. A cosine schedule set for 100k steps but stopped at 50k is a different schedule from one set for 50k, and can give a worse loss [4]. So the decay has to be tied to the length of each run, not copied from a longer one. Warmup-stable-decay schedules [26] make this easier: runs of the same model can share the stable phase, with a short decay branched at each training budget you want to measure.

    When the step count changes, the learning rate usually should too. At a fixed token budget, doubling the batch halves the number of steps. A common rule of thumb is to keep the product of learning rate and steps roughly constant, as a rough way to preserve cumulative update scale. That gives the linear scaling rule, learning rate proportional to batch size. For Adam, an SDE analysis gives a square-root learning-rate scaling rule, together with adjustments to the momentum coefficients and epsilon [27]. Treat both as starting points, not laws, and note that beyond the critical batch size [28], increasing the batch gives diminishing reductions in the steps needed to reach a target loss and worsens compute efficiency. The simplest way to avoid the question is to fix the batch size in tokens across candidates and let it grow along the ladder by one stated rule.

    Weight decay has the same issue. With AdamW and a constant learning rate, the decay per step is lr × wd, giving a decay timescale of about 1 / (lr × wd) steps when that product is small. If both lr and wd stay fixed while runs get longer, that timescale becomes a shrinking fraction of training. Wang and Aitchison study keeping the timescale roughly constant in epochs as model and dataset size change [29]. Whatever rule you choose, apply it to both ladders.

    Parametrization

    Rule: use μP to transfer the learning rate tuned at the bottom rung across width.

    Matched tuning budgets at every rung are expensive. Under the standard parametrization, the best learning rate drifts left as width grows, so each rung needs its own sweep. The maximal update parametrization (μP) changes how initialization and learning rates scale with width so that the optimum stays approximately stable and you can tune at a smaller model [22][23].

    A useful intuition comes from how sums scale. Take a hidden layer y = Wx, with W of shape d × d and x a vector whose entries are of order one. The initialization and the weight update both multiply x, but their contributions add up differently.

    Random sum: the initialization. W starts with independent entries of standard deviation σ. Each output coordinate yi = ∑j Wijxj is a sum of d terms with random signs. Like a random walk, its typical size is σ√d. To keep activations of order one at any width, set σ = 1/√d. This is the familiar fan-in initialization.

    Coherent sum: the update. For one example, the gradient of the loss with respect to W is an outer product g xT, where g is the gradient at the layer's output. For intuition, approximate Adam by a single sign-normalized step, ignoring momentum history and epsilon. Then ΔW has entries of order η, with the sign pattern of −g xT. The change in the output is then Δyi = ∑j ΔWijxj ≈ −η sign(gi) ∑j |xj|. Every term has the same sign, so the sum grows like d, not √d. To keep the feature change of order one, the learning rate must be η ∝ 1/d. This illustrates the coherent contribution; the full μP analysis handles batches and training history [22][23].

    In this single-example picture, the update is a rank-one matrix aligned with the input; the random initialization has no preferred direction. That is why one scales as 1/√fanin and the other as 1/fanin. Both rules are needed: initialization keeps the first forward pass stable, and the learning-rate rule keeps every later step stable.

    The scaling depends on which dimension grows. For a Transformer with hidden width d and Adam, one μP convention uses the following width scalings, up to fixed multipliers, with untied input and output weights [23]:

    Width scalings for Adam with untied input and output weights; fixed multipliers omitted.
    LayerShapeInit stdAdam learning rate
    Input (embedding)vocab × d1/√fanin (≈ constant)η
    Hidden (attention, MLP)d × d, d × 4d, ...1/√faninη / fanin
    Output (unembedding)d × vocab1/faninη / fanin

    An embedding lookup selects a row; its fan-in is fixed as width grows, so neither its init nor its learning rate needs width scaling. Hidden layers follow the two rules above. The output layer sums over d, so its learning rate scales as 1/d; its initialization is shrunk further, to 1/d, so that the random logits at initialization vanish as width grows and the learned part of the output dominates. When head width grows, μP scales the query-key dot product by 1/dhead rather than 1/√dhead, up to a fixed multiplier, to control correlations that can develop during training [23].

    In practice you fix a base width d0, usually the bottom rung, tune η there, and set the hidden and output learning rates to η d0/d at larger widths. The learning rate, and several other hyperparameters, then transfer across width without retuning [23].

    Two caveats. The derivation is for Adam; with SGD the gradient itself changes size with width, and the table differs [22]. And μP addresses transfer across width. Transfer across depth needs its own treatment [24], and transfer across training tokens and batch size is approximate, so the largest rung is still worth a check against a small sweep.

    Optimizer

    Rule: same optimizer, same hyperparameter scaling rules, same conventions on every ladder, and treat a change of optimizer as its own experiment.

    This sounds obvious, but the details leak. Adam's epsilon is easy to overlook. As gradient magnitudes approach it, epsilon can dominate the denominator and suppress updates in some layers [30]. Weight decay can be coupled to the learning rate or not, and the two conventions give different effective decay. Gradient clipping thresholds interact with μP, since the per-layer gradient scale is part of what μP sets. Fix all of these once and write them down.

    Newer optimizers such as Muon [31] change the update geometry per layer, which is exactly the quantity the μP derivation reasons about, so a candidate architecture trained with Muon and a baseline trained with AdamW are two changes, not one. If the optimizer is part of the proposal, give it a ladder of its own, with the baseline architecture, so the gain from the optimizer and the gain from the architecture can be read separately.

    A bit of history: why compute-fair comparison faded

    Collected while writing this post. Not needed to use it, but fun.

    None of this is new. In 1993, Cortes and colleagues fitted power-law learning curves on small training sets and extrapolated to the full database, explicitly because training on the full set was too expensive [5]. In 2001, Banko and Brill showed that another order of magnitude of data changed which algorithm won [6]. In 2017, Hestness et al. measured power-law exponents across translation, language modelling, vision and speech [7]. In 2018, Lucic et al. gave nearly every GAN variant a large enough budget and watched them all reach the same FID [8]. Kaplan et al. made the curves famous [3], and Chinchilla corrected them [4]. In vision the control sat in every table: ResNeXt was built to match the FLOPs and parameters of the ResNet block it replaced, so that cardinality was the only thing that moved [9]; RegNet compared whole design spaces under fixed FLOP regimes [10]; and Green AI asked that FLOPs be reported as a first-class result [11].

    The best advertisement for controlling the variables is the Chinchilla story itself. Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. disagreed on how to split a budget between parameters and tokens. Porian et al. reproduced both and traced the gap to three loose variables: whether the last layer's compute was counted, the warmup length, and whether the optimizer was re-tuned at each scale [12]. Chinchilla's own parametric fit did not survive a replication attempt either [13]. If this happens at DeepMind and OpenAI, it happens in your sweep.

    So why did a routine discipline drop out of education? My guesses. The unit of experiment changed: from 2015 to 2020 everyone trained on ImageNet from scratch for 90 epochs, so FLOPs and parameters were the natural axes; today most academic work starts from someone else's checkpoint, the compute that went into it is invisible, and people stopped counting. Baselines became too expensive to retrain under matched compute, so numbers are copied from papers trained on different data for a different number of steps, and the comparison is by citation. Leaderboards have no compute column. And the people who built the habit moved to industry, while the people entering the field learned architectures, not experimental method. The Physics of Language Models series is the modern counter-example: controlled synthetic data, one factor changed at a time, repeated [14][15]. It reads like a physics paper because it is run like one.
